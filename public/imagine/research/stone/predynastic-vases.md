# Predynastic Egyptian hard-stone vases

**What's there.** Predynastic Egyptians (Naqada I-III, c.4000-3100 BCE) made [stone vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_vessels_in_ancient_Egypt) in basalt, diorite, porphyry, breccia, schist and gneiss, alongside softer travertine. The Petrie Museum holds hundreds from elite graves at Naqada, Abydos, and Hierakonpolis. The single largest cache is later: Djoser's Step Pyramid subterranean galleries, excavated by Cecil Firth and J.E. Quibell in the 1920s-30s, held an estimated 30,000-40,000 vessels, many predating Djoser by centuries. Separately, the tri-lobed "[Disc of Sabu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabu_disk)" (schist, 61cm diameter, c.3000 BCE) is a one-off whose function is still undetermined. And in the Petrie Museum sits "Core 7" — a tapered granite drill core Petrie pulled from Giza in 1881-82 — the physical exhibit at the center of the whole tube-drilling debate.

**How mainstream archaeology explains it.** Denys Stocks's replicative work — bow-driven copper tube drills with quartz-sand slurry, stone pounders, sandstone rubbers — reproduced Egyptian-style shaping and hollowing, at roughly 800 hours per hardstone vessel, matching striation patterns on real originals. The strongest recent evidence is a [2025 npj Heritage Science paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-02196-7) that laser/CMM-scanned 19 authenticated Petrie Museum predynastic vessels against 25 modern lathe-made and 24 modern handmade comparators: the ancient pieces showed good outer concentricity but poor circularity — a grinding signature statistically close to modern *handmade* replicas, not the tight, consistent tolerance a lathe leaves.

**The alternative claim.** Engineer Christopher Dunn's core argument rests on Core 7's spiral groove, which he says advances ~0.100 inch per revolution — [too fast](https://www.gizapower.com/petrie.html), he argues, for hand-pressure abrasive drilling, implying powered, bearing-mounted cutting. A separate viral claim — that Petrie recorded a Djoser-gallery vase wall 1/40 inch (0.635mm) thick — has [no traceable citation](https://veronicasart.com/high-precision-vases-under-djosers-step-pyramid-fact-vs-fiction/) in the actual Firth/Quibell excavation reports; the thinnest verifiable figure is ~2mm, describing alabaster paneling, not a hardstone vase.

**Round 2 traced the "precision vase" claim to its actual origin.** It starts with a 2017 [structured-light scan](https://www.artifactfoundation.org/og-vase) of collector Adam Young's "OG Vase" — a piece with no excavation record, only a style-based date guess. The viral 2023 UnchartedX scan credits "professional metrologists" Alex Dunn and Nick Sierra, working with Christopher Dunn in an Illinois lab; no independent credentials for either surfaced in a search, which reads as an enthusiast project rather than an institutional one. Every vessel cited as showing CNC-grade precision traces to just [two private collections](https://veronicasart.com/tracing-the-source-the-provenance-of-ancient-egyptian-artifacts/) (Young, Matt Beall) with no verified findspot — while excavated museum pieces show ordinary hand-tool variance.

**Two independent reversals, the same pattern.** Cryptographer Mark Qvist's original 2023 analysis reported radial deviations as tight as 3 microns and concluded the vase needed "5 axes of freedom" to produce; after a higher-resolution rescan in 2025 he [reversed himself](https://veronicasart.com/egyptian-stone-vases-high-precision-or-complete-delusion-research-proves-one-thing/), now calling the piece "definitely not 5,000 years old." Separately, researcher Fomitchev-Zamilov's own January 2025 CT study of 27 vessels originally read some as machine-lathed — [his post now carries his own retraction](https://maximus.energy/index.php/2025/01/03/astonishing-precision-of-predynastic-egyptian-stone-vessels-a-metrological-study/), superseded by his later peer-reviewed npj Heritage Science paper (round 1). Two sympathetic analysts, working independently, both moved toward the skeptical conclusion once they gathered more data — a real, if informal, form of convergence.

**The debate isn't as closed as round 1's framing suggested.** A [May 2026 UnchartedX post](https://unchartedx.com/site/2026/05/20/precise-vases-are-fakes-again/) directly disputes the npj paper, arguing its "no machine signature" metric measures circularity alone and ignores wall-thickness consistency, and citing the Artifact Foundation's own Petrie Museum scans showing sub-100-micron results on *museum* pieces too. Neither side's raw numbers have been checked by a disinterested third party — even the strongest peer-reviewed rebuttal is now being contested by the same community it rebutted, unresolved as of this round.

**Open question.** No credentialed outside metrologist — not affiliated with either the enthusiast-scanning community or the fringe rebuttal side — has audited a raw scan file or a scanner's calibration record from either camp. The 2025 metrology study's 19-vessel museum sample also still skews toward ordinary pieces, not the rarest, thinnest-walled forms fringe claims focus on.

**What would settle it.** A fresh, disinterested lab re-running the npj paper's method against both museum pieces and the disputed private-collection vases, with wall-thickness (not just circularity) included in the quality metric and calibration records published alongside the data — closing the gap the May 2026 rebuttal actually points at, instead of another self-published exchange.
