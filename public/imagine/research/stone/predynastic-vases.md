# Predynastic Egyptian hard-stone vases

**What's there.** Predynastic Egyptians (Naqada I-III, c.4000-3100 BCE) made [stone vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_vessels_in_ancient_Egypt) in basalt, diorite, porphyry, breccia, schist and gneiss, alongside softer travertine. The Petrie Museum holds hundreds from elite graves at Naqada, Abydos, and Hierakonpolis. The single largest cache is later: Djoser's Step Pyramid subterranean galleries, excavated by Cecil Firth and J.E. Quibell in the 1920s-30s, held an estimated 30,000-40,000 vessels, many predating Djoser by centuries. Separately, the tri-lobed "[Disc of Sabu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabu_disk)" (schist, 61cm diameter, c.3000 BCE) is a one-off whose function is still undetermined. And in the Petrie Museum sits "Core 7" — a tapered granite drill core Petrie pulled from Giza in 1881-82 — the physical exhibit at the center of the whole tube-drilling debate.

**How mainstream archaeology explains it.** Denys Stocks's replicative work — bow-driven copper tube drills with quartz-sand slurry, stone pounders, sandstone rubbers — reproduced Egyptian-style shaping and hollowing, at roughly 800 hours per hardstone vessel, matching striation patterns on real originals. The strongest recent evidence is a [2025 npj Heritage Science paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-02196-7) that laser/CMM-scanned 19 authenticated Petrie Museum predynastic vessels against 25 modern lathe-made and 24 modern handmade comparators: the ancient pieces showed good outer concentricity but poor circularity — a grinding signature statistically close to modern *handmade* replicas, not the tight, consistent tolerance a lathe leaves.

**The alternative claim.** Engineer Christopher Dunn's core argument rests on Core 7's spiral groove, which he says advances ~0.100 inch per revolution — [too fast](https://www.gizapower.com/petrie.html), he argues, for hand-pressure abrasive drilling, implying powered, bearing-mounted cutting. A separate viral claim — that Petrie recorded a Djoser-gallery vase wall 1/40 inch (0.635mm) thick — has [no traceable citation](https://veronicasart.com/high-precision-vases-under-djosers-step-pyramid-fact-vs-fiction/) in the actual Firth/Quibell excavation reports; the thinnest verifiable figure is ~2mm, describing alabaster paneling, not a hardstone vase. Notably, one fringe-sympathetic analyst who argued unprovenanced "ultra-precise" private-collection vases showed CNC-grade geometry [later reversed himself](https://veronicasart.com/egyptian-stone-vases-high-precision-or-complete-delusion-research-proves-one-thing/) after 2025 CT scans, concluding those specific pieces are likely modern or reworked.

**Open question.** The 2025 metrology study is reassuring but its 19-vessel sample skews toward ordinary museum pieces, not the rarest, thinnest-walled, most complex outlier forms — which are exactly the ones fringe claims focus on. Nobody has yet published a full-time-cost replication of those specific extreme forms.

**What would settle it.** Full experimental replication of the thinnest-walled, most geometrically complex outlier vessels, in comparable time and finish to the mainstream toolkit — plus CT/laser scans with clean, documented chain-of-custody (unlike the disputed private-collection pieces Dunn and others cite) would close the remaining gap either way.
