# Sacsayhuamán and Cusco's polygonal masonry

**What's there.** [Sacsayhuamán's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacsayhuam%C3%A1n) largest limestone blocks are commonly cited at 100-200 tonnes (sources disagree on the exact figure — only the multi-hundred-ton magnitude is consistent); Pedro Cieza de León, writing in the 1550s, described stones "twelve feet and more than twenty long." Dating is historical, not radiocarbon-of-the-stone: the monumental phase is tied to the reigns of Pachacuti, Topa Inca Yupanqui, and Huayna Capac (15th century) via Inca oral tradition recorded by Spanish chroniclers, with Cieza recording roughly 20,000 laborers on the project. Cusco's famous [12-angled stone](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/man-vandalizes-famed-12-angled-stone-an-inca-engineering-marvel-in-peru-180986092/), on Calle Hatun Rumiyoq, actually predates Sacsayhuamán's main building phase — it's part of Inca Roca's palace (c.1350-1380). It was vandalized in February 2025.

**How mainstream archaeology explains it.** The field's anchor is Jean-Pierre Protzen's decade-plus of on-site experimental replication at Ollantaytambo, published as *Inca Architecture and Construction at Ollantaytambo* (Oxford University Press, 1993). His masons-eye-view: pound stone with harder hammerstones — river cobbles, hematite, dense basalt — then repeatedly lower, mark the high-contact points, lift, re-pound, and re-test until blocks mate by friction and weight alone. No mortar, no iron, no wheel. Microscopy backs it up: joint surfaces show pounding micro-fractures, not the smooth finish thermal or laser cutting would leave. One genuine caveat worth naming: the famous "can't fit a knife blade" claim is [an overgeneralization](https://theperuchannel.com/cuscos-12-angled-stone-reality-and-mythology/) — some elite faces really are that tight, but visible hand-wide gaps exist elsewhere in Inca walls, including on the 12-angled stone itself.

**The alternative claim.** A real, published minority hypothesis: chemist Helmut Tributsch's 2017 peer-reviewed paper proposes Inca masons used a reddish, bacterially-produced acidic mud to chemically soften volcanic rock for fitting — grounded in colonial references to a now-vanished substance, but never demonstrated at construction scale. Separate from that is the popular fringe claim (Brien Foerster, *Ancient Aliens*) of a lost plant-derived liquid that melts stone, or laser/machine cutting — for which no plant, machined surface, or vitrification has ever been found; petrographic study shows only pounding-consistent fracture patterns.

**Open question.** Shaping and fitting is well demonstrated experimentally. What isn't: how blocks were hauled tens of kilometers over Andean terrain and lifted onto terraces several meters high. Protzen's own fieldwork is strongest on quarrying and surface-shaping, weaker on large-block transport and lifting mechanics — that logistics gap is real, even though nobody credible attributes it to lost technology.

**What would settle it.** A full-scale replication of moving and setting a 100+ ton block with only period technology — rope, ramps, log rollers, manpower — on comparable terrain. Direct chemical testing of in-situ joint surfaces for residues matching the Tributsch hypothesis. And any independently verified vitrification or machine-tool mark at the actual quarries, which a century of open site access has not produced.
