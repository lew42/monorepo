# The Great Pyramid's granite coffer

**What's there.** In the King's Chamber of the Great Pyramid sits a single block of Aswan red granite, hollowed into a coffer: external [89.62 x 38.50 x 41.31 inches](http://home.hiwaay.net/~jalison/gpkc.html), internal 78.06 x 26.81 x 34.42 inches, wall thickness roughly 5.8-6.9 inches, commonly cited around 3.75 tons. Flinders Petrie's 1883 survey took 681 measurements at six-inch intervals — the primary source behind every number on this page. Popular accounts round its external-to-internal volume ratio to "exactly twice"; Petrie's own figures (16.22 vs 8.22 cubic cubits) give about 1.97:1, close but not exact. At 89.6 inches long, the coffer is too large to fit through the Ascending Passage or the chamber's own entrance — it had to be set in place during construction, before the passages and roof were finished.

**How mainstream archaeology explains it.** Petrie himself first proposed the mechanism: straight bronze/copper saws and tube drills carrying fixed jewel points (likely corundum), inferred from marks on this coffer and on a granite drill core he numbered "Core 7" — the same core covered on this program's [predynastic-vases](./predynastic-vases/) page. Modern replication supersedes the jewel-point detail while confirming the marks: Denys Stocks's bow-driven copper tube drills, fed with wet sand, cut cores ["identical in profile"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287348456_Experiments_in_Egyptian_archaeology_Stoneworking_technology_in_ancient_Egypt) to Petrie's Giza cores, explaining the spiral striations as sand grains jamming and scoring, not a hard point. And Petrie's own 1883 account is refreshingly candid about the process being hand-guided, not flawless: he documents the masons cutting a saw groove too deep near the coffer's north end, backing out, and cutting too deep *again* two inches lower.

**The alternative claim.** This coffer is the object at the center of Christopher Dunn's *The Giza Power Plant*, and it carries his single most specific, checkable claim across his whole body of work: he measures the external saw-groove spacing at ~0.050 inch and a tube-drill spiral advance of ~0.100 inch per 6 inches of circumference, and argues the grooves cut *deeper* through the granite's harder quartz crystals than through its softer feldspar — the reverse of what abrasive grinding should produce — concluding the only fit is powered, ultrasonic-style machining. No independent lab has replicated or refuted this specific quartz-versus-feldspar observation.

**Open question.** Nobody has published a 3D scan of the coffer itself. The 2020s ScanPyramids-era non-destructive surveys (ground-penetrating radar, muon tomography) targeted structural voids like the North Face Corridor, not surface measurement of this artifact — so every precision claim about it, Petrie's and Dunn's alike, still rests on hand tools and feeler gauges from the 1880s and 1990s. It's the same metrology gap as the Serapeum boxes, for the same reason: nobody has pointed a scanner at it.

**What would settle it.** A published 3D scan of the coffer's interior and exterior, checked specifically against Dunn's quartz-versus-feldspar groove-depth claim, would confirm or kill his most falsifiable point outright. Re-deriving Petrie's volume-ratio and wall-thickness figures from that same scan would settle whether "exactly twice" is real design intent or a rounding of 1.97.
